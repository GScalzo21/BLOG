# My First Cybersecurity Job Interview: A Reflective Recap

About a month ago, I had my very first cybersecurity job interview. It was for an **IT DevOps Analyst** role with a well-established **S&P 500 real estate investment trust (REIT)**—a rare opportunity, and certainly not something I was expecting. Truthfully, I couldn’t even remember applying for the position, but once I got the email, I figured I’d take it as a sign to get some formal interview experience outside of law enforcement. I already had a feeling that the job might not align with my current focus, but I wanted to push myself to try something unfamiliar and see what I could learn from the process.

Leading up to the interview, I was surprisingly calm. I felt prepared—not overly confident, but definitely focused. The nerves didn’t take over like I thought they might. Instead, I welcomed the challenge. One thing that stood out to me: the interviewer joined the call about eight minutes late. While I understand delays happen, punctuality is something I really value, especially when it comes to professional opportunities. I showed up early, prepared, and focused, which I believe reflects how I’d approach any role I step into.

The interview itself had a fast-moving structure, mostly technical, and it covered a range of topics. I was asked about my knowledge of **Microsoft Azure**, scripting with **PowerShell**, familiarity with **DevOps tools**, and my hands-on experience with **SIEM platforms** like **Microsoft Sentinel**. They also asked how I would respond to discovering a **critical vulnerability** on a production server. I explained my approach to prioritizing the issue, communicating with stakeholders, and implementing a remediation plan.

Another question dove into a scenario where an application's functionality didn’t align with its documentation. I shared an experience from my internship involving phishing detection in **Defender XDR**, where I noted a mismatch between alerts and expected outcomes, documented the gap, and supported improvements to the detection logic. Rather than bluffing, I leaned on my real-world practice and emphasized my willingness to learn quickly and adapt.

Post-interview, I reflected a lot on how tech interviews differ from what I was used to in law enforcement. It’s a different energy—a bit more structured and technical, of course—but I enjoyed seeing how those conversations flow. Even though I haven’t heard back and I’m assuming I didn’t get the offer, I don’t regret taking the interview at all. It gave me confidence, helped me better understand how to prepare, and reminded me that I can hold my own in a completely different field.

If I had received the offer, would I have taken it? Possibly. With the distance, it would’ve had to be worth my while—not necessarily in terms of a huge paycheck, but in feeling like I could truly contribute and grow with the company. I wouldn’t mind taking a small pay cut if I knew I was stepping into a team that valued growth, learning, and long-term development.

For now, I’m continuing to focus on my **internship with Log(N) Pacific**, sharpening my skills in **vulnerability management** and **threat detection**. I’m also working toward my **Microsoft SC-200 certification**, building projects and documenting my learning journey through my [GitHub portfolio](https://github.com/GScalzo21).

---

Thanks for reading—and if you’re also breaking into the field, don’t underestimate the value of just going for that first interview. Even if it’s not “the one,” it’s a major step forward.
